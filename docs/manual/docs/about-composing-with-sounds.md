**Recognising Sources** – {#RECOGNISE} An initial consideration when composing with sounds is how recognisable to make the source sounds. This can be important when the sound design is referencing something in 'real life'.

Sometimes the sound design aims to put together a recognisable real-life scenario, either to create a particular sonic environment such as an urban or countryside setting, or to create sound effects such as doors closing, autos screeching around corners, and the like. Here the decisions concern how much to enhance the natural sounds.

For example, in the film *Titanic*, the doors between compartments are closed against the onrushing water. To make this more dramatic, the sound designer, Gary Rydstrom, combined the sound of a heavy bin on a truck rolling on a track with a ratcheting sound, and then the clang of doors closing on an old ship mixed with a prison door being banged shut (*Audio Media* Jan 1998, p.66).

Footsteps might be completely natural or foley-recorded by someone stepping on a tray of gravel. On the other hand, the sound of high-heeled shoes on a marble floor might be further processed to make them sharper, more percussive, while some reverb/echo creates an empty/lonely environment. These are very important decisions based on many considerations about dramatic purpose and context.

However, in a great deal of electroacoustic music, there is minimal or no source recognition. The sounds have become abstracted from their origins, and their sonic nature and shape are woven into the formative principles of the composition. This is why so many of the CDP sound design functions at first seem so strange, and one asks "Why make these sounds?" Grasping the role of abstract formative principles in music composition is a big step, but at times it is essential. I have added these notes about 'composition' to provide, I hope, some insight into what this is all about.

**Designing the Supplementary Files –** {#FILEDESIGN} A great deal of the composing when using the CDP software takes place in the design of the supplementary files. The variety and detail that can be put into a **note data file** for a TEXTURE program is a good example. Similarly, there is the design of the different types of breakpoint file: for changes in **pitch** (movement in vertical space), for **panning** (movement in horizontal space), for the **density** (and/or scatter) of multi-event texturing (TEXTURE or BRASSAGE/*Grainmill*), for **tuning** a sound to a chord with spectral tuning or time-varying **filtering** (etc.).

The time-varying 'automation' produced by these files is an essential ingredient of the suppleness of a living, dynamic musical image. Similarly, the **mixfile** designs the placement and timing (and levels) for a number of sounds, some already with their own panning design. Sometimes this just builds a sound complex by placing the sounds on top of each other, and sometimes there will be a whole sequence of separate and overlapping sounds. Then there can be mixes of mixes such that a resulting passage of music can be many layers of mixes deep.

Given the semi-algorithmic nature of many of the CDP processes, particularly the TEXTURE Set which in effect does a great deal of internal mixing, but also other complex operations such as shuffling and weaving, it is in fact relatively easy to create intricate results 'on only one track' as it were, and this includes vertical mixing. Then horizontal mixing puts together a whole set of files to create a passage of musical sound design.

**Music and 'Passage' –** {#PASSAGE} What makes music music? On the one hand there is 'articulation': *how* a performer plays a sound, a living process of producing the sound and personal projection. In sound design, this can mean the visceral impact of the sound itself, whether this is for example soft and gentle or bitingly acrid. Iannis Xenakis is a composer who never lost sight of the nature and sheer physical impact of his sounds, no matter how much he employed mathematics and computers.

On the other hand, there is the question of how time is being shaped, and here there is a direct connection with human perception. This brings us to the concept of music as 'passage'.

The author Suzanne Langer (*Feeling and Form*) is my favourite author about music: how it works, what it means. I want to say something about this here to try to give some pointers about how working with sounds can produce real music. There are many different views about these matters. These are what seem most helpful to me. (I quote from the Scribner's edition of 1953).

Langer writes that "*The purpose of all musical labor, in thought or in physical activity, is to create and develop the illusion of flowing time in its passage, an audible passage filled with motion that is just as illusory as the time it is measuring.*"(p. 120). This image of 'flowing time' is not real life, it is a symbolic form that is itself expressive. Both the composer and the performer enter into this symbolic form and put their energy and commitment into realising it as fully as possible.

The feeling involved is not the composer's or performer's autobiographical feeling but the feeling inherent in the form itself which has an objective character outside the artist. There may be overlaps with personal experience; this can enhance but should not distract from a clear perception of the form. Thus Langer writes "*The matrix ... which establishes the greatest rhythm of the piece and dictates its scope, is born of the composer's thought and feeling, but as soon as he* [or she!] *recognizes it as an individual symbol and sets forth its outline it becomes the expression of an impersonal Idea, and opens to him* [or her], *a deep mine of musical resource.*" (pp. 122-123). And: "*Music begins only when some formal factor – rhythm or melody* [or sound object!] – *is recognized as a framework in which accent and intonation are elements in their own right, not chance attributes of individual speech.*"(p.142).

**Purpose –** {#PURPOSE} What I am suggesting therefore is that, among many different motivations to compose, the *purpose*, the aim of the activity is to create an objective symbolic form so that the intention is actually realised in the form. It is quite possible to *intend* to create a certain feeling or whatever, and for the form itself to contradict this intention.

Thus it is important to be open to the objective nature of the musical symbol, to look for the 'framework' in which components can live and grow, to listen to the flow, to listen *at tempo* to what is there and feel what *it* is like. Then it starts to become something that exists in its own right as 'music', whether made of combinations and different proportions of melody, harmony, rhythms and sounds. Note that very often, an idea for a piece of music will begin with the rhythm, the very essence of the flow.

**Time Patterns –** {#TIMEPATTERNS} One way to approach the task of composition is to study and think about *time patterns*: that is to say, the nature of the flow. A few examples:

- the rate of information flow: such as a great deal in a short amount of time, contrasting with very little in a lengthy amount of time
- layered combinations of rapid and slow
- gradual transition *vs.* sudden contrast
- gradual accumulation, such as an orchestral crescendo *vs.* gradual dispersal and fragmentation
- repetition *vs.* variants of a figure or sound
- symmetrical or proportional arrangements *vs.* asymmetry
- gradual unfolding, assembly or gathering, *vs.* sudden appearance
- the focused *vs.* the inchoate, ambiguous or blurred
- the presence of dialogue between parts contrasting with strong, dominant themes
- rapid, driving, edgy *vs.* slow relaxed and smooth

Patterns such as these can be seen to be universal in the sense that they can appear in any type or culture of music. To some degree, they are the components of a musical form. An effort to observe them can enrich musical experience, deepen the understanding of musical styles, provide ideas for new compositions, and help in assessing one's own work. The task is to listen deeply to your own musical creation so far, to experience it, and let your imagination conjure some kind of symbolic understanding. Then you take it from there.
