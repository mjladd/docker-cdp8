# About Sound Design

*by Dr Archer Endrich*

*A brief look at what working with sound involves*

|---|---|---|

Sound design is about making and transforming sounds for various purposes. In the music of past generations, it was present as the occasional special sound effect, but later on through creative forms of orchestration. In our time, it has become a mainstream component of the musical universe, mostly for films, but also for many other media and occasions. What is different now is that all kinds of sounds are used *as sounds*. Highly inventive means are used to make sounds for radio, there is the electronic generation of sound, and the alteration of existing sounds *musique concrete*, which is what the CDP software does.

For further study about sound design, the text *Sound Design - The Expressive Power of Music, Voice, and Sound Effects in Cinema* by David Sonnenschein (Michael Wiese Productions, 2001) is a goldmine of information for anyone working with sound. Besides providing technical information about sound, it deals with sensation, perception, mood, story context, the voice, and aspects of musical structure. I strongly recommend it.

After making some observations about the context in which a designed sound may be placed, the section below discusses two topics that bring out the nature of CDP's powerful tools for realising sound design in an expressive, musical way: texture & randomisation and time-varying.

**Context for a sound effect –** {#CONTEXTS} Sound design can take place in a number of different contexts. Sometimes the context is an abstract piece of electroacoustic composition in which sonic shapes and processes become the primary consideration. When sound is being designed for an animation or film, elements of story, scene, mood and cultural resonances become relevant to the nature of the sounds created. David Sonnenschein's book has many examples. The 'Final Cut' articles in the journal *Audio Media* discuss the how and why of the sound design in a given film. Sound is a subtle and enveloping medium which induces felt responses in an infinite variety of ways. Similarly, sound created for an installation also implies a context based on the nature and purpose of the installation. A sound designer needs to be aware of and responsive to these elements of context in the choice of source sounds and in the nature of the effects produced. The various techniques for manipulating sound never really exist in a vacuum.

**Texture and randomisation –** {#TEXTURES} Texture in the sense of a multi-event complex became an important structural component of 20th Century music, both standard instrumental and electroacoustic. Stockhausen's *Gruppen* (1955-1957) and *Momente* (1965) are just two examples. However, when electroacoustic music emerged, texture became a natural ingredient, especially when combined with varying degrees of randomisation. The latter provided a supple way to select and place events, not only quickly, but also in a way which suited sound material. Natural equivalents are the croaking of hundreds of frogs, the calls of geese on the wing, footsteps, conversations made by groups of people, the rustle of the wind stirring the leaves of a tree. Such sounds would seem oddly regimented if they were set out in regular rhythms.

The CDP TEXTURE set of programs is therefore an important component of the sound designer's toolbox. Not only can it create lush, randomised multi-event textures from one or more sounds, but it can also mix randomised features with fully-defined features, such as rhythmic templates, harmonic grids, nodal substructures, and one or more motifs. To give a taste of what TEXTURE can do, here is a sound made from a stroke on a [girder](../sounds/girder1gdc.wav), time-stretched and lowered by two octaves: [girder1gdcx4c2dtd24.wav](../sounds/girder1gdcx4c2dtd24.wav). It makes a richly timbral starting point for a multi-event texture. To create this texture with TEXTURE SIMPLE, *packing* is 2 seconds (with a half-second *scatter*), the pitch range is 48 to 72 MPV (each event will be anywhere within 2 octaves), and the duration of the segments is set to be between 4 and 6 seconds. The resulting texture is [girder1gdcx4c2dtd24tx.wav](../sounds/girder1gdcx4c2dtd24tx.wav).

'Surface' texturing by means of segmentation or distortion is applied to a single sound, changing it from rough to smooth or *v.vs.* – rather than creating a multi-event complex. CDP has many options for segmentation and distortion. Partly, the effects alter the emotional tone of a sound, and partly they start to remove the ability to recognise the source of the sound: how it was produced, what made it. Thus it is part of a process of abstraction, turning the original sound into a musical sound object with generic rather than specific associations. When and to what degree to do this is usually guided by context, as above.

**Time-varying –** {#TIMEVARYING} Music needs to be flexible, supple, ever-changing. Even very repetitive 'minimalist' music is not as repetitive as it may at first seem. Underlying processes of change and transformation, as well as randomised rhythmic relationships are taking place. The CDP software has responded to this need by making most of the program parameters able to change over time. This is done with *time value* breakpoint files. When a sound is made with such files operating, the output is accordingly modified over time. Other software refers to such processing as 'automation'. The breakpoint files can be created by typing the values into a text file, or by means of a graphic editor to draw lines and points. CDP's (extended) breakpoint files can create exponential or logarithmic curves as well as straight lines. It is even possible with the CDP software to create breakpoint and other text files algorithmically using a programming language such as Python, TCLTK, or CDP's *Tabula Vigilans*.
